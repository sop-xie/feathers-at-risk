Feathers at Risk

One in eight bird species is threatened with extinction. Which ones?

Each circle below is a group of birds, shaded by the share of its species threatened with extinction. Click into an order, then a family, to see its species, each colored by its 2024 IUCN Red List status. The danger isn't spread evenly: it piles up in some families and skips others entirely.

Project Prototype Writeup

What have you done so far?

In this project, we wanted to see which bird species are threatened with extinction, and whether that comes down to their traits or to the families they belong to. We started by merging two bird databases into one table of about 10,900 species. AVONET gave us body measurements, habitat, and diet, while BIRDBASE gave us each species' 2024 IUCN Red List status, whether it breeds only on islands, and its clutch size. The first name join silently dropped every species whose genus or spelling was different between the two sources, so we built a reviewed name-fix table and a script that rebuilds the merged file from the raw downloads. The main visualization is a zoomable D3 circle pack where orders open into families and families open into species, and each group is shaded by the share of its species that are threatened. Clicking on a species opens a profile with a Wikipedia photo, its traits, and a map of where it has been recorded in GBIF. The buttons above the chart let you pick out seabirds, island-only breeders, birds over 1 kg, or flightless birds, which fades every bird that doesn't match and compares the matching birds' threatened share with everyone else's. Before landing on the atlas, we sketched a few other designs, including a taxonomic tree and some other chart variations. We went with the circle pack because it was the only design that let readers compare species within a single family.

What will be the most challenging part of our project to design, and why?

The hardest part to design is going to be separating the effect of a trait from the effect of a family. Close relatives tend to share both their traits and their risk, so a chart showing that heavy birds are more threatened might really just be showing that a few heavy-bird families, like albatrosses, are in trouble. This shows up in our data, since breeding only on islands predicts risk inside 68 of 81 families, but body mass only does so in 50 of 88. The atlas already groups species by family, but we still need the filters and annotations to point readers toward the pattern inside each family instead of just the overall color. Our second challenge is the scrollytelling we have planned, where all of the roughly 10,900 species dots move between layouts. Animating that many SVG circles at once already slowed our filter clicks down by one to three seconds. So we will most likely move those transitions to canvas, and the catch is that hover and click still have to work once the dots are drawn there.

Our code and data are on GitHub at sop-xie/feathers-at-risk.

Is it the family, or the traits?

Close relatives share their traits as well as their risk, so a chart that says “big birds are threatened” might really be saying “a few big-bird families are threatened.” Zooming into a single family lets you compare species that differ in a trait but share the same ancestry.

Island breeding passes that test. Among rails and crakes, the species that breed only on islands are far more likely to be threatened than their mainland relatives.

A note on the data

These are correlations, not causes: island breeding and large bodies leave species more exposed to threats like invasive predators and hunting. Gray rings mark the 12 species in the data already extinct or surviving only in captivity; species flagged “possibly extinct” stay Critically Endangered, as the Red List lists them.
